## A city of contrasts where sun-soaked beauty meets urban reality

So you’re dreaming of living in Valencia? Those photos of palm trees, beaches and stunning architecture have you convinced it is paradise on Earth.

Grab a horchata and let us share some inconvenient truths about life in this Mediterranean jewel. Don’t worry: like a good paella, every seemingly negative ingredient adds to the final flavour. The table near the end sums up each downside and its silver lining.

## The symphony of noise (and poor insulation)

![Symphony of noise](https://mikebastin.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/01/symphony-of-noise.jpg)

Those elegant facades with ornate balconies and traditional tiles are essentially sophisticated tambourines. The insulation is so poor that every conversation, TV show and late-night phone call next door becomes a shared experience.

And then there’s Fallas. For most of March the city turns into a war zone of celebration: explosions at 08:00 (the traditional “despertà”), random fireworks all day, and the daily 14:00 mascletà that makes your windows rattle like they’re trying to escape. Your pets will be hiding under the bed from 1 to 19 March.

The silver lining: thin walls and festive chaos create a unique form of community. You’ll never feel alone, you’ll learn to sleep through almost anything, and you get front-row seats to some of the most spectacular fireworks in Europe from your poorly insulated balcony.

## The urban obstacle course

![Valencia streets](https://mikebastin.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/01/valencia-streets.jpg)

[Walking through Valencia](https://mikebastin.com/live-in-valencia/) can feel like an obstacle course designed by a mischievous urban planner. First the dog deposits, then the mysterious early-morning puddles (spoiler: not all are from rain).

Graffiti artists treat every clean wall as a blank canvas. Some tags are genuinely artistic; others look like someone sneezed while holding a spray can.

![Valencia Frida ](https://mikebastin.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/01/frida-kahlo-graffiti-valencia-1024x1024.jpg)

The upside is one of the most impressive street cleaning operations in Spain. The cleaners are local celebrities, out every morning erasing the previous night’s adventures. The city is a giant Etch A Sketch, reset daily.

## The job hunt safari

![Job Search Valencia](https://mikebastin.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/01/job-search-valencia.jpg)

[Looking for work in Valencia](https://mikebastin.com/valencia-remote-working/)? Hope you enjoy sending CVs into the void. Unless you’re in tech or tourism, or have a very specific set of skills, the job market can feel as welcoming as a vegetarian at a paella contest. Salaries make you wonder about a decimal point error in your contract (there isn’t one).

The silver lining? A resilient, creative workforce. Everyone has a side project, co-working spaces are popping up faster than churro stands, and you quickly learn that happiness is less about earnings than about long lunches with friends.

## The language labyrinth

![Valenciano Spanish](https://mikebastin.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/01/valenciano-spanish.jpg)

Got kids? For years, public schools here taught mainly in Valencian, which surprised parents who expected Castellano. It was like ordering a paella and getting fideuà: still delicious, just not what you expected.

Since the 2024 education freedom law, families choose the base language of schooling, Valencian or Castellano, with the new model fully in place from the 2025 to 2026 school year. Both languages and English stay on the timetable, so your children can become trilingual without really trying.

> The law establishes the right of families to choose the base language of schooling, adapted to the linguistic zones of the Comunitat Valenciana, and repeals Law 4/2018.
>
> Source: [BOE, Ley 1/2024, de 27 de junio, de la Generalitat, por la que se regula la libertad educativa](https://www.boe.es/buscar/doc.php?id=BOE-A-2024-16511)

The school buildings might remind you of your own school days, several decades ago, and allocation seems to involve complex algorithms and possibly ancient astrology.

## The local connection challenge

![Valencia social life](https://mikebastin.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/01/valencia-social-life.jpg)

Valencians are a close-knit club with a waiting list longer than the queue at the City of Arts and Sciences on a rainy Sunday. They are people of habit, rooted in family traditions and social circles, and some see newcomers as the reason for rising rents and the “turistification” of traditional neighbourhoods.

Getting in can feel harder than finding a parking spot in El Carmen. But once you’re in, you’re family: grandmother’s birthday, the cousin’s wedding, the secret recipes and the favourite hidden bars. The initiation might last decades; the loyalty is eternal.

## Traffic, bikes and the lights

![Valencia traffic](https://mikebastin.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/01/valencia-traffic.jpg)

Whoever designed Valencia’s traffic system must have loved puzzle games and adrenaline sports. Roundabouts like Plaza de España are less traffic circles than gladiatorial arenas: hesitate, and you circle forever like an electron in an urban atom.

Once an extremely [car-centric city, Valencia has made admirable strides](https://mikebastin.com/living-in-a-flat-in-valencia-a-pragmatic-overview/) toward becoming bike-friendly, quirks included. The new lanes are narrow, shared by cyclists, lightning-fast e-scooters and pedestrians who treat them as optional pavement. The crown jewel is the Turia Gardens, the old riverbed park that lets you cover a large part of many cross-town journeys car-free, and more bike-priority streets appear every year.

The upside? After a few months you could thread a bicycle through a crowd with the precision of a neurosurgeon.

### A city-wide light show

Valencia has roughly 1,100 signal-controlled junctions, reportedly the second-highest density per resident in the world after New York and the highest in Europe, about one traffic light per 750 residents.

> Valencia has 1,100 regulated intersections, one traffic light for every 750 citizens, second in the world only to New York and first in Europe.
>
> Source: [El Periòdic, "València es la ciudad con más semáforos por habitante de Europa y la segunda del mundo", 13 July 2022](https://www.elperiodic.com/pvalencia/valencia-ciudad-semaforos-habitante-europa-segunda-mundo_837397)

The pedestrian warning blink gives you roughly two seconds to sprint like Usain Bolt. Then there is Spain’s flashing amber:

On a more positive note, Valencia made [headlines in 2016 by introducing female figures on their traffic lights](https://english.elpais.com/elpais/2016/03/08/inenglish/1457429892_421779.html), timed with planned LED upgrades so the change cost nothing extra. Some debated the dresses, but the aim was to make women more visible in public space.

## Petty crime: mind your belongings

Serious crime is not what worries residents, but petty theft is another story. Growing tourist crowds have attracted light-fingered opportunists who see unattended phones and dangling handbags as open invitations. The classic is the phone snatched from a café table.

Bikes? Anything less than two heavy-duty locks is a donation to the local theft economy. The hotspots are predictable: the beach promenade, Plaza de la Reina, the Central Market, crowded terraces and even picnics in the Turia Gardens.

**Violent crime against residents remains rare.** The best defence is mild paranoia: belongings close, phone closer.

## Dogs rule the city (sometimes a bit too much)

Valencia seems to have a dog in every other household, including large breeds in modest flats. The social side is brilliant: dog walks turn into friendships sealed with a warm “¡Hasta luego!”.

Not everything is rosy. Dog mess is a persistent problem, owners now have to dilute urine with water and vinegar, and balcony dogs provide an unsolicited canine chorus. Picture the aroma during an August heatwave.

Spain’s 2023 animal welfare law has stepped things up with [new regulations](https://www.protectapet.eu/animal-protection-law-in-spain): mandatory third-party liability insurance for dog owners, [training courses for owners](https://www.surinenglish.com/spain/these-are-the-most-important-changes-that-20230822143759-nt.html) once the implementing regulation sets them out, and fines ranging from €500 to €200,000 for breaches.

> Minor infringements carry a warning or a fine of €500 to €10,000, serious ones €10,001 to €50,000, and very serious ones €50,001 to €200,000. Dog owners must hold third-party liability insurance and complete a free training course.
>
> Source: [BOE, Ley 7/2023, de 28 de marzo, de protección de los derechos y el bienestar de los animales, articles 30 and 76](https://www.boe.es/buscar/act.php?id=BOE-A-2023-7936)

## The climate conundrum

![Valencia weather](https://mikebastin.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/01/valencia-weather.jpg)

Summer is a terracotta oven set to “Mediterranean max”, with everyone moving from shade to shade like solar-phobic ninjas. Those poorly insulated buildings store heat, so your flat becomes a sauna that runs around the clock.

Winter, contrary to the brochures, exists: not the bone-chilling cold of Northern Europe or North America, but a damp chill that settles straight into your bones. Still, the climate has built an enviable outdoor culture, with terrace cafés handing out blankets in January.

## The bureaucratic ballet

![Valencia burocratic nightmare](https://mikebastin.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/01/valencia-burocratic-nightmare.jpg)

Paperwork here needs the patience of a saint and documentation to rival the British Library. The classic catch-22: you need an NIE to open a bank account, and the NIE process seems to need one too. Scoring an appointment is like winning the lottery, though rumour says refreshing at precisely 08:00 on a Friday helps.

_Personal note: one does wonder if the system is designed to nudge us towards a gestor, a bureaucratic wizard who can materialise appointments faster than you can say “¿otra vez?”_

Our Latin American friends have smoother sailing: their documents are already in Spanish, and various agreements fast-track their paperwork. For the rest of us, the upside is zen-like patience, a brilliant culture of expats helping newcomers, and the feeling that after Spanish bureaucracy, everything else in life is easy.

## The final verdict

Here is every trade-off at a glance:

| The downside | The upside |
|---|---|
| Thin walls and Fallas | Community, and fireworks from your balcony |
| Dirty streets at night | Spotless streets by morning |
| A tough job market | Side projects and long lunches |
| A closed social circle | Loyal friends once you are in |
| Chaotic traffic | The Turia and a growing bike network |
| Petty theft | Little violent crime |
| Slow bureaucracy | Patience, and help from other expats |

Valencia isn’t perfect, but neither is any city. What makes it special is how these flaws weave into its character. You are not just relocating to a city; you are adopting a lifestyle, quirks and charms included.

If you can embrace the chaos, love the late lunches, accept that time is a suggestion and that relationships matter more than efficiency, you might fall for this imperfect paradise. **Just don’t expect anything to happen quickly**, except maybe those roundabout encounters.

Bring earplugs, patience and a sense of humour. **You’re going to need them all!**
