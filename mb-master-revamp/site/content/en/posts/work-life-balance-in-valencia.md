## Working in Valencia: a guide for international professionals

We came to Valencia with a business to grow and a worry that a city famous for long lunches would slow it down. It did the opposite. Morning client calls sit comfortably beside beachside lunches, and networking happens over horchata in Plaza de la Reina as easily as in an office.

Valencia's pace is not only a lifestyle choice; used well, it helps a business. Below we set out the local working day, how we manage clients across time zones from here, and answers to the questions newcomers ask us most.

## How the Valencian working day runs

The biggest adjustment for us was not the language or the bureaucracy. It was the timetable.

Traditional Valencian businesses start early, around 08:00. Between roughly 10:30 and 11:30 comes almuerzo, the mid-morning break for a bocadillo, usually eaten at a local bar with colleagues. It powers people through long mornings that run until 14:00, which makes sense once you know dinner is often at 21:00 or 22:00. For more on the city's food and festivals, see our partner site's guide to [things to do in Valencia](https://valenciamove.com/things-to-do-valencia/).

Lunch then runs from about 14:00 until 17:00, with an optional but widely respected siesta. International companies and startups often use a condensed day instead, and many expat business owners mix the two.

| Part of the day | Traditional Valencian | International company | Our hybrid |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Start | 08:00 | 10:00 | 09:00 |
| Mid-morning | Almuerzo, 10:30 to 11:30 | Working | Client calls |
| Lunch | 14:00 to 17:00 | One hour | 14:00 to 15:00 |
| Finish | Offices open until about 20:00 | 18:00 | 18:00 |

The local timetable is not about working less. It spreads the day around the city's rhythm, and as a business owner you can choose which version suits you. The [expat community in Valencia](https://valenciamove.com/expat-community-valencia/) is a good place to compare notes on what works.

## Why time zones work in our favour

Working with clients abroad from Valencia turns the local schedule into an advantage. Our day starts at 09:00, in step with most of Europe, and we use the almuerzo window for international calls, when European clients are at their desks and keen on mid-morning meetings.

![a sandwich with onion rings and a small flag](https://mikebastin.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/01/valenciano-almuerzo.jpg)

A shorter lunch from 14:00 to 15:00 keeps international projects moving while still giving us a taste of the lunch culture. We finish around 18:00, just as Spanish counterparts head into their final meetings, which leaves the evening free for beach walks, terrace dinners and meeting local entrepreneurs who are still in their offices until 20:00.

Being slightly out of step with the local rhythm lets us serve both sides. Clients abroad expect [content written for their market](https://mikebastin.com/services/multilingual-content/), while local customers still find us through a [well-kept Google Maps listing](https://mikebastin.com/blog/how-to-promote-your-local-business-on-google-maps/).

## Managing clients across time zones

Our calendar used to look like a puzzle. After years of refinement, a few rules keep the business flowing and our [remote working routine in Valencia](https://valenciamove.com/blog/valencia-remote-working/) intact.

### Set windows and batch by region

European clients know they can reach us during their normal business hours, and clients in Asia know we take early morning calls. We batch communication by region, and go further with a time zone buffer: whole days for one region rather than juggling several in one day. Tuesdays and Thursdays are our European days, with calls between 09:00 and 17:00 CET; Wednesdays may start early for Asia and end late for the Americas.

| Client base | When we talk | What to know |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Europe | 09:00 to 17:00 CET, Tuesdays and Thursdays | Most calls land during almuerzo |
| Asia | Early mornings | Book those days separately |
| US East Coast | From about 16:00 Spanish time | Catches their early starters |
| US West Coast | Evenings | Keep it to set days |
| UK | Any time in our morning | An hour behind, so a sunny walk comes first |
| France | Around 14:00 to 16:00 CET | Peak hours fall in the Spanish lunch break |

### Use tools, not arithmetic

We use World Time Buddy to see overlapping business hours, and Calendly set to specific time zones so clients book without manual conversion. Calendar apps showing several zones at once stop mistakes when juggling clients from Stockholm to Dubai. Our [AI consulting for small businesses](https://mikebastin.com/services/ai-consulting/) often starts with exactly this kind of scheduling automation.

### Be open about where you work

Being clear about our location and hours has strengthened client relationships rather than weakened them. Many clients value the cultural range we bring and respect the boundaries we set. The siesta hours, 14:00 to 17:00, often become our most productive focus time, as European clients wind down. ## Finding your own rhythm

Some expats adopt the Spanish timetable fully; others keep their home country's hours. Many, like us, build a hybrid: quiet mornings for focused work, a shorter lunch than locals, and an early enough finish to join the evening paseo along the beach. For the practical side of settling in, read about [renting a flat in Valencia](https://valenciamove.com/blog/living-in-a-flat-valencia/), and for working across cultures, our piece on [cultural differences in multilingual websites](https://mikebastin.com/blog/cultural-differences-in-multilingual-websites/).

The aim is not to force yourself into any one schedule, but to find the rhythm that serves your business and still lets you enjoy the quality of life that brought you here.

## Questions newcomers ask us

### How do we network locally when international calls fill the usual hours?

Try early networking events such as business breakfast meetups, which run before the workday from about 08:00 to 09:30. Many international business groups in Valencia organise them for exactly this reason. Keep one evening a week for local networking, on a day with fewer international commitments.

### What if we cannot make the traditional long lunch meetings?

Suggest a breakfast meeting at 09:00 or a merienda meeting, the afternoon snack, between about 17:30 and 18:30. Many Spanish professionals welcome these times, especially those with international clients. Weekend brunches and informal Friday evening gatherings work too.

### How do we explain an unusual schedule without seeming unprofessional?

Present it as a strength: your international connections give local partners longer business hours and insight into other markets. Be clear about your availability and flexible for important meetings. Many Spanish businesses want international reach and value partners who have it.

### How do we build relationships outside the traditional schedule?

Put quality ahead of quantity. Attend major industry events and business fairs, which often run flexible hours, and join professional WhatsApp groups and LinkedIn communities. Weekend business and cultural events are common in Valencia and good for building relationships.

### What about impromptu meetings late in the Spanish afternoon?

Set boundaries and offer alternatives. Explain your schedule up front and suggest mornings between 09:00 and 11:00, a productive time for Spanish professionals before almuerzo. For urgent matters, offer a short video call or meet the next morning. Most colleagues respond well to a proactive approach.
