## Is Valencia a good place to live? The honest truth for expats

You’ve probably seen it splashed across expat forums and lifestyle magazines: Valencia, crowned “best city in the world to live.” And look, there’s real truth behind the hype. But if you’ve actually packed your bags and moved here (like many of us have), you know the picture is a bit more nuanced than the glossy Instagram version.

So, is **Valencia a good place to live**? Short answer: **absolutely yes**, but with a few caveats that could make or break your experience, depending on your lifestyle, career situation, and tolerance for paperwork that seems designed to test your sanity.

Valencia offers something rare: Mediterranean charm mixed with modern infrastructure and a pace of life that genuinely prioritises _being_ over _doing_. But the transition isn’t always smooth.

Between dealing with the labyrinthine bureaucracy and surviving your first August humidity, there are things every expat wishes someone had told them _before_ signing that lease. Let’s get into it.

## The 5 biggest pros: why Valencia keeps topping the charts

There’s a reason people from the UK, the US, and Northern Europe keep flocking to the banks of the Turia. Here are the five pillars that make Valencia genuinely special.

### 1\. Your money goes a lot further

For many expats, the main driver is financial freedom, and Valencia delivers. Yes, inflation has hit Spain like everywhere else, but compared to London, Paris, or even Madrid and Barcelona, life here is remarkably affordable. A cappuccino averages about €2.40.

A spacious flat in a trendy neighbourhood? Still within reach without selling a kidney, though rents have climbed fast since 2020.

Here’s a quick comparison to put things in perspective, with both cities priced in euros:

| Expense | London (UK) | Valencia (Spain) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1-bedroom apartment, city centre (monthly rent) | €2,552 | €1,252 |
| Three-course meal for two, mid-range restaurant | €93 | €60 |
| Monthly public transport pass | €200 | €35 |

We’ve crunched all the numbers in our [detailed guide to the real cost of living in Valencia](https://mikebastin.com/valencia-cost-of-living/). It’s worth a read before you start budgeting.

### 2\. Around 2,700 hours of sunshine a year

The weather alone is worth the move for many people. Spain’s weather agency records around 2,700 hours of sunshine a year in Valencia, and winters are short and mild.

> Valencia climate normals, 1981 to 2010: 2,696 hours of sunshine a year; January averages a daily high of 16.4°C.
>
> Source: [AEMET, standard climate values for Valencia](https://www.aemet.es/es/serviciosclimaticos/datosclimatologicos/valoresclimatologicos?l=8416&k=val)

You might grab a light jacket in January, but you’ll still see locals having lunch on outdoor terraces. After years of grey Northern European skies, that constant Vitamin D boost does wonders for your mood and energy levels. You’ll find yourself walking more, cycling more, and actually _wanting_ to go outside.

### 3\. Culture that goes way beyond the beach

Valencia isn’t just a beach town. It’s a cultural powerhouse. The futuristic City of Arts and Sciences, the UNESCO-listed Fallas festival in March, live music, street art, independent galleries… there’s always something going on.

But the city’s greatest asset might be the **Jardín del Turia**, a stunning 9 km park that runs through a diverted riverbed right through the heart of the city. Locals cycle, jog, and picnic there daily. It becomes part of your routine without you even trying.

### 4\. Healthcare that actually works

Spain consistently ranks among the top countries globally for healthcare, and Valencia is no exception. If you’re contributing to social security, you get access to excellent public hospitals. Many expats also go the private route, and here’s the kicker: decent private health insurance costs a fraction of US premiums, with quick access to specialists.

Coming from the NHS waiting lists or the US healthcare system, that feels almost unreal. Premiums rise with age, so get quotes early.

### 5\. A city where you actually feel safe

Safety catches a lot of newcomers off guard. Valencia is _incredibly_ safe. You’ll see children playing in plazas at 22:00 while their parents enjoy a glass of wine nearby.

Walking home alone at night? Not the anxiety-inducing experience it can be in many other European cities. The sense of community and security is one of the biggest reasons families fall in love with the place.

## The 5 biggest cons: what nobody warns you about

If we stopped at the pros, we wouldn’t be doing our job. Here’s the stuff that can catch you off guard if you’re not prepared.

### 1\. Bureaucracy that will test your soul

Bureaucracy is the number one complaint from every single expat, and for good reason. Getting your NIE (tax identification number), registering on the _padrón_, opening a bank account… every step feels like it was designed by someone who actively enjoys queues. The famous _mañana_ attitude is charming when you’re waiting for a beer.

It’s infuriating when you’re trying to legalise your residency. Our honest advice? Budget for a [good relocation consultant](https://mikebastin.com/valencia-relocation-services/), your sanity will thank you.

### 2\. You need to learn Spanish (no, really)

You can get by with English in touristy spots, but _living_ here is a different story. You’ll need Spanish for everything from fixing a broken boiler to understanding your lease.

And here’s an extra twist: Valencia has two official languages: Spanish (Castellano) and Valenciano (the Valencian variety of Catalan). Most locals speak Spanish to foreigners, but official signs, school documents, and government paperwork often come in Valenciano.

The good news? Even basic effort goes a long way.

We put together a guide on [essential Spanish phrases for Valencia](https://mikebastin.com/basic-spanish-valencia/). Locals genuinely appreciate it when you try.

### 3\. The local job market is tough

If you’re planning to find a local job, temper your expectations. Salaries in Valencia are significantly lower than in the UK or US, and unemployment remains above the EU average.

For [remote workers, digital nomads](https://mikebastin.com/valencia-digital-nomads/), and entrepreneurs? Valencia is paradise. But breaking into the local market is hard unless you have specialised skills and fluent Spanish. The “work-to-live” balance is fantastic, just don’t expect to get rich.

### 4\. August will humble you

Everyone loves the sun until a July heatwave pushes past 35°C. Unlike Madrid’s dry heat, Valencia’s coastal location makes summers feel heavy and sticky.

Many locals actually flee the city in August for cooler mountain retreats. If your apartment doesn’t have air conditioning, July and August can be genuinely miserable.

And while we’re at it, the city can also be surprisingly noisy. Between the echoey streets and the local love affair with fireworks, silence is a luxury.

We actually wrote [a whole piece about Valencia’s “50 shades of noise”](https://mikebastin.com/valencia-50-shades-of-noise/). It’s more serious than it sounds.

### 5\. The Spanish schedule takes getting used to

Lunch at 14:00. Dinner at 21:30. Many small shops closed from 14:00 to 17:00.

If you’re used to eating at 18:00, you’ll find the best restaurants closed. The _siesta_ schedule means running errands during your lunch break is often impossible.

It’s one of those things that sounds charming in theory but takes real adjustment in practice. After a few months, though, most people wouldn’t go back.

## Who is Valencia perfect for, and who should think twice?

### You’ll love Valencia if:

-   **You work remotely:** Great internet, a growing coworking scene, and a timezone that overlaps with both US East Coast mornings and European business hours.
-   **You have a young family:** Parks everywhere, incredible safety, and an outdoor culture that turns the whole city into a playground.
-   **You value lifestyle over career ladder:** The Mediterranean philosophy of “work to live” will fit you like a glove.
-   **You love good food:** The local markets (especially Mercado Central) offer some of Europe’s best fresh produce at prices that’ll make you smile.

### You might struggle if:

-   **You’re chasing a corporate career:** The local corporate ladder is short, and the rungs can be slippery.
-   **You have zero patience for inefficiency:** A delayed bus or a slow government clerk will test you. Regularly.
-   **You really hate the heat:** Ten months of sun sounds great until you’re craving a crisp autumn morning.
-   **You want to live in an English bubble:** Unlike parts of the Costa del Sol, Valencia is a real Spanish city. You need to engage with the language and culture to thrive.

## How to test the waters before you commit

Never decide based on a one-week holiday in July. Living somewhere is fundamentally different from vacationing there. If you’re seriously considering whether Valencia is right for you, here’s our recommended approach.

### 1\. Book a mid-term rental

Skip the hotel. Get an Airbnb or a “digital nomad” flat for at least a month, ideally in a residential neighbourhood like _Arrancapins_ or _Benimaclet_ rather than the tourist-heavy _El Carmen_.

You’ll get a real feel for the supermarkets, the commute, and the noise levels. Our [guide to the best neighbourhoods in Valencia](https://mikebastin.com/best-neighborhoods-valencia/) can help you choose.

### 2\. Come in the off-season

Visit in November or February. See if you still love the city when the beach isn’t the main draw. You’ll also experience the “real” Valencia without the summer crowds, and get a much better sense of whether this place fits your day-to-day life.

### 3\. Do some real “life admin”

Try performing a mundane task: visit a local pharmacy for a specific product, or inquire about a gym membership. It’s a quick reality check on the language barrier and the level of service you can expect.

### 4\. Research the neighbourhoods properly

Every _barrio_ has its own personality. Ruzafa is trendy and buzzy; El Carmen is historic and a bit cramped; the area around the City of Arts and Sciences feels modern and spacious. Picking the wrong neighbourhood can completely colour your opinion of the city.

## So, is it worth it?

Valencia is a city of layers. On the surface, it’s all oranges, sunshine, and futuristic architecture. Dig deeper and it’s complex, occasionally frustrating, but deeply rewarding.

If you come with an open mind, a bit of financial cushion, and the willingness to learn the language, you’ll likely find the pros far outweigh the cons. The “good life” here isn’t about luxury. It’s about the luxury of _time_.

Time for a long lunch. Time for a walk through the Turia. Time to watch the sunset without the frantic rush of a capital city buzzing in your ear.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is it expensive to live in Valencia?

Compared to most Western European and North American cities, no. Rent is your biggest expense, but food, transport, and leisure are significantly cheaper than the UK or US.

Prices have been creeping up, though, in popular expat areas like Ruzafa and the city centre. For the full breakdown, check our [Valencia cost of living guide](https://mikebastin.com/valencia-cost-of-living/).

### Do we need to speak Spanish to live in Valencia?

To survive? Not really. To actually thrive and build a life?

Yes. You’ll need Spanish for admin tasks, medical appointments, and building real relationships with your neighbours. Even [learning the basics](https://mikebastin.com/basic-spanish-valencia/) will drastically improve your experience.

### Is Valencia safe for expats?

Very. Valencia is considered one of the safest cities in Europe.

Violent crime is rare, and it’s a great environment for solo women and families. Just keep an eye on pickpockets in crowded tourist spots, as you would anywhere.

### What’s the biggest challenge for expats in Valencia?

Bureaucracy, hands down. Getting your residency, setting up utilities, dealing with any government office.

It’s slow, repetitive, and requires specific documentation. A [relocation service](https://mikebastin.com/valencia-relocation-services/) can save you weeks of frustration.

### How is the healthcare in Valencia for foreigners?

Excellent. If you’re working and paying into the system, you have access to public healthcare. Those on certain visas or not yet working usually take out private insurance, which is high-quality and much more affordable than what you’d pay in the US.
