Your translated site is live, visitors from the new market arrive, and very few buy. Usually nothing is broken. The site simply reads as foreign: the tone is off, the prices look wrong, the form rejects a local address, and the visitor goes back to a competitor who feels local.

Below are ten website localization mistakes that cause it, grouped by where they happen, with the fix for each.

## Language and culture

### Ignoring cultural differences

Content that sells in one country can feel off, or even offensive, in the next. Culture shapes tone, colours, imagery, humour and buying behaviour. [Cultural adaptation services](/services/multilingual-content/) fit the content to each audience's expectations before it goes live.

### Translating word for word

A literal translation is grammatically correct and commercially flat: it loses tone, intent and local relevance, and it dilutes the brand. [Transcreation](/services/translation-services/) adapts voice, style and message to the local culture instead.

### Failing to adjust imagery

The wrong photo tells a visitor the page was made for somebody else. Icons, gestures, dress codes and family representations vary widely between cultures.

### Letting the brand voice drift

Localize the message without losing the personality. A style guide every translator follows keeps your positioning intact in every language.

## Search and technical

### Overlooking local SEO and translating the keyword list

A translated page does not rank on its own. Each market has its own search behaviour, phrasing and sometimes its own preferred search engine. [Multilingual SEO](/services/multilingual-seo/) starts with keyword research done in the target language, not a translation of the English keyword list.

### Neglecting mobile

Some markets are mobile-first, others still lean to desktop, and a slow or cramped page loses visitors in both. Make every language version responsive and fast.

### Skipping testing

Every defect you skip testing for gets found by a customer instead. [Test every localized version](/blog/localisation-testing-tools/) before launch: translation quality, navigation, form fields, payment gateways and speed, across browsers and devices.

## Law, payments and user experience

### Ignoring local laws

Non-compliance costs you fines and trust in the same stroke. Each market has its own rules on data protection, cookie consent and ecommerce, so make sure the privacy policy, terms and checkout follow local requirements.

### Not adapting payment methods and formats

If people cannot pay the way they prefer, they abandon the basket at the last step. The same goes for dates, numbers and currency displayed in an unfamiliar format.

| Element | UK | Germany | Netherlands |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Currency | £ | € | € |
| Number format | 1,000.50 | 1.000,50 | 1.000,50 |
| Common payment | Cards | PayPal | iDEAL |

### Overlooking user experience

One design does not suit every region, and friction shows up as bounce rather than complaints. Localize navigation, calls to action and form fields (postcode and address formats differ too).

## Where to start

Website localization is a strategic investment, not a technical chore. Start with the mistakes closest to the sale (payments, forms and trust), then work outwards to search and culture.
