## Why the EUDR changes how you source from Vietnam

You buy rubber, coffee, wood or furniture from Vietnam, and from 30 December 2026 a large or medium EU importer has to prove where each raw material was grown before the goods can go on sale. Your suppliers are sound; the work now is bringing their paperwork up to the same standard, and much of the evidence sits with smallholders and processors your team has yet to meet.

Starting now keeps every shipment saleable in the EU. Below: what the EU Deforestation Regulation (EUDR) asks of you and when, what tracing a Vietnamese supply chain involves, seven agencies and advisers that can do the groundwork, and the part that decides many audits, which is language.

## What the EUDR requires, and when

Read the dates and the product list closely, so you know early whether you are in scope. The EUDR bars certain commodities and the products made from them from the EU market if they are linked to deforestation or forest degradation after 31 December 2020. It replaces the EU Timber Regulation and sits within the European Green Deal.

It was first due to apply on 30 December 2024 and has been postponed twice. The revision agreed in December 2025 fixed the current dates, and the Commission's simplification review in May 2026 confirmed them as they stand.

### Products covered

The seven commodities are cattle, cocoa, coffee, oil palm, rubber, soya and wood, plus listed products derived from them, such as chocolate, paper and furniture. The July 2026 delegated act changes the list of derived products and keeps the seven commodities: it removes items including cattle hides and leather, retreaded tyres and articles of vulcanised rubber, and adds soluble coffee and certain palm oil derivatives. Check your HS codes against the current annex.

### What importers must do

- **Risk assessment**: identify the deforestation risk attached to each product and its supply chain.
- **Geolocation**: supply coordinates of the plots where the commodity was produced, to show the land has been deforestation-free since the cut-off.
- **Legality**: confirm production complied with the laws of the country of origin, including land use rights.
- **Due diligence statement**: file a declaration through the EU Information System before placing goods on the market, backed by evidence.

## What tracing a Vietnamese supply chain involves

In Vietnam, the task is proof. Its forests are a minor source of deforestation for EUDR commodities, so the underlying risk is lower than in some other producing countries; a supplier passes on a documented clean origin, so the documents carry as much weight as the origin itself. The table sets out where the tracing work lies and what a local partner does about it.

| Challenge | Why it matters for the EUDR | What a sourcing partner does |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Fragmented chains | Rubber, coffee, cocoa and timber pass through many smallholders and subcontractors | Maps each tier and names the sub-suppliers |
| Little digital record keeping | Plot coordinates and harvest dates are rarely held digitally | Collects GPS data on site and trains producers in simple tools |
| Paper documentation | Even larger exporters keep inconsistent paper files | Converts records into auditable digital files |
| Complex land law | Overlapping jurisdictions and varied readings of land rights | Brings in local legal checks on titles and land use |
| Imported inputs | Many components still arrive from China, adding another origin to prove | Traces inputs back beyond the Vietnamese factory |
| Weak intermediaries | An agent's EUDR knowledge decides how early gaps are seen | Replaces guesswork with scheduled audits and reporting |

Traceability systems, producer training and audits all add cost. Companies that build them early also end up with a supply chain they can defend to a customs authority.

## Prepare for EUDR compliance step by step

Every step below costs least when done before the deadline. In order:

1. **Map the supply chain early.** List every supplier and production site behind each product, down to the raw material. The finished map is what shows you the risk areas.
2. **Build traceability systems.** Use digital tools that capture geolocation data, land use evidence and supplier certificates, and keep them in one auditable place.
3. **Bring suppliers with you.** Compliance depends on the producers. Give them training, clear guidelines and a named contact.
4. **Audit regularly.** Independent audits on the ground remain the most reliable way to keep deforestation-linked material out of the chain.
5. **Work with a partner who knows the country.** A local agency bridges the regulatory, cultural and technical gaps, in Vietnam as in Thailand or Indonesia.
6. **Treat it as a long-term investment.** The short-term cost buys continued access to the EU market, and readiness when similar rules spread.

Vietnam's growing use of certified sourcing, such as FSC timber and sustainable rubber, makes it a lower-risk option for importers who do this groundwork.

## The language gap in EUDR audits

The answer that decides compliance is often given in Vietnamese, so an audit needs someone in the room who understands it. EUDR compliance is presented as a sourcing problem and an audit problem. Both are real. After over two decades in translation and language services, we would add a third: it is a language problem.

The conversations that decide whether a supplier passes due diligence happen between EU compliance officers and Vietnamese plantation managers, smallholders, processors and officials. Many of them work only in Vietnamese, regional varieties, and sometimes minority languages from the Central Highlands or the Mekong Delta.

A qualified interpreter lets your auditor talk to the person who knows where the rubber, coffee or cocoa came from, who is usually someone other than the best English speaker in the chain. The strongest audits we see pair a sourcing consultant with a Vietnamese-English interpreter who knows supply chain and agricultural terminology. The cost is small next to what it protects: a whole batch of due diligence can turn on one term about land tenure or harvest dates.

### Why machine translation needs a human editor

A phone running Google Translate or DeepL is tempting; a human editor is what keeps the meaning intact. Vietnamese is tonal, and the tone decides the meaning of a word. Agricultural and land use vocabulary also varies by region, in ways a specialist reads far more reliably than a general-purpose engine.

The line between _rừng tự nhiên_ (natural forest) and _rừng trồng_ (plantation forest) decides whether a parcel is compliant at all, and we have seen audits compromised by translations that confused the two. A trained interpreter is the one who catches it.

### Why less-resourced languages gain value as AI spreads

The languages your suppliers speak are the ones where AI has the least to learn from, which makes the human specialist more valuable as AI spreads. Large language models learn from web text, which is overwhelmingly English. They perform well on English to French, Spanish or German, where training data is plentiful, and less well where good bilingual data is scarce. Vietnamese sits in the middle: many speakers and plenty of everyday content, but little high-quality bilingual material in regulatory or agricultural fields. AI handles tourist Vietnamese well and needs a human for the Central Highlands, where much of the country's coffee and rubber is grown.

> The Central Highlands account for 92% of Vietnam's coffee cultivation area and 90% of its national coffee output.
>
> Source: [USDA Foreign Agricultural Service, "Coffee Annual: Vietnam", GAIN report VM2025-0018, 19 May 2025](https://apps.fas.usda.gov/newgainapi/api/Report/DownloadReportByFileName?fileName=Coffee%20Annual_Hanoi_Vietnam_VM2025-0018)

The same applies to Khmer, Lao, Malay, Tagalog, Burmese, Bahasa Indonesia and the many indigenous languages that matter for sourcing across South-East Asia. As AI takes over routine translation, what is left for people is the high-stakes specialist work, and that is where compliance sits. ## Four decisions to make before December

Four decisions to make now, while there is still time before the December deadline:

- **Budget for a qualified interpreter** on every on-site audit, including field visits to plantations, farms and processing sites, not only the headline meetings.
- **Have compliance documents professionally translated**, with certification through a Vietnamese notary office (_phòng công chứng_) where the document requires it. The cost difference over machine translation is small, and the legal protection is substantial.
- **Use AI translation for triage**, such as sorting incoming supplier emails, and keep binding contract language and final compliance files with human translators.
- **Consider a bilingual sourcing consultant** who covers both the consultant and the interpreter roles. People who understand both the EUDR and Vietnamese supply chains are rare, and worth the premium.
